## The invitation problem

Common Ground was a three-day program of public talks, neighborhood tours, and workshops about how a city makes decisions. Its initial announcement read like a conference agenda. Participants who already knew the organizers could decode it, but a first-time reader could not tell where to begin or what they were expected to contribute.

I led the campaign strategy and helped the team connect promotion with what happened at the event. The program lead owned the schedule. A designer and a volunteer editor translated the strategy into print and social material.

## Listen at the point of hesitation

We traced the invitation from a flyer or group chat through to arrival. In short conversations at a library and a neighborhood market, people rarely questioned whether the subject mattered. They asked how long it took, whether they could come alone, and whether they would be expected to speak. Those questions gave the campaign its vocabulary.

I gathered the questions into four moments: notice the invitation, imagine going, choose a first event, and find your way in. Each touchpoint had a different job. The street poster needed a single promise. The sign-up page needed plain language about time, access, and what to bring.

## Put the first visit on the poster

We considered a campaign organized by topic and one organized by venue. Both helped people with a plan. I proposed a third route based on how much time they had. A visitor could see what fit a lunch break, an evening, or a Saturday, then follow the same line from the poster to the program.

The design turned that choice into a visual device: branching blue paths that rejoined at a recognizable shared mark. I wrote the invitation and mapped the paths. The designer made the print system and the editor adapted it for partner channels.

## Carry the promise through the door

The team made a route-based poster set, a compact event finder, and a host sheet with the same language as the campaign. At events, hosts asked first-time visitors what brought them in and whether anything had almost stopped them. We used those notes to revise a late reminder and add clearer information about arriving without a group.

The useful result was editorial and operational. The next program brief started with a visitor journey, and the team kept the same map from campaign review through event delivery. Arrival feedback helped us refine directions and onboarding. Comparing which channels drove attendance would require another study.

## What I’d test next

I would compare a time-based invitation with a plain event list at the same partner locations and collect arrival feedback in both groups. That would give the route idea a clearer test than comparing two different days or audiences.
