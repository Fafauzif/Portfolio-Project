## The brief and the team

Our three-person team had two days to address a gap in rail travel. We picked the connection between a seat reservation and actually finding a suitable seat. I ran the journey-mapping exercise and made the first prototype. A teammate researched carriage layouts and another led the final pitch.

## Start with the rider’s next decision

Existing carriage diagrams looked precise but asked riders to interpret direction, door position, and seat orientation at once. We prototyped a diagram-first view and a route-first view that began with the appropriate carriage door. During quick tasks, the door-first version was easier to use after selecting a seat.

We carried that order into the proposal and put the diagram behind the core instructions. Access requirements can differ on the return trip, so the experience asks for them again when a traveler sets up a separate booking.

## Follow the reservation through boarding

Our final concept paired a reservation detail and a printed boarding strip. The panel shortlisted the idea and asked what information station staff could see. We added an assistance handoff to the concept. For a station test, I would check whether a traveler can use the boarding strip to recover when a train stops with its carriages in reverse order.
