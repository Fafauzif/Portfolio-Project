## Count uses a map can miss

The same stretch of waterfront served as a quiet walking route in the morning, a place to fish after work, and an informal drop-off for small vendors. Meeting notes collapsed that variation into a single category called recreation.

I organized repeated walks at different times and gave the team a shared way to record what we saw. The client’s planner ran formal engagement. My contribution was the learning plan and the evidence maps used to compare the activity patterns.

## Make different needs visible at once

An early map grouped comments by respondent. That was useful for accountability and less useful for design. I created a second view that grouped notes by time of day and by constraint: access, informal storage, shade, or path conflict. It exposed places where a small physical change could affect several groups in different ways.

## Keep a record of disagreement

Our final atlas kept unresolved trade-offs alongside areas of agreement. That moved the client conversation from “What does the public want?” to “Who gains or loses access if this gate moves?” We handed over the atlas and prompts for a further round of listening. The atlas informed the next design brief, while construction decisions remained open.
