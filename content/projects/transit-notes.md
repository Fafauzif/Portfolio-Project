## Locate the decision

During a delay, Bramble gave riders a technically accurate service status. It rarely answered the decision commuters had to make at the stop. I joined the service team to work out which information belonged at each point in that journey.

In accompanied rides and station interviews, we asked people to walk through their next decision as they saw an update. A cancellation mattered less than whether the rider knew the nearest stop with a usable onward connection.

## Make a board that answers the next question

We mapped three moments: check if the route is running, decide whether to wait, and find a fallback. I paired early incident wording with stop-level signage. Dispatch pointed out where the fallback details could change before publication. We made that uncertainty visible by adding the timestamp alongside the alternative.

Instead of redesigning the entire network map, we prototyped a status panel that linked to a printed local strip. During a tabletop simulation, the strip gave the station team a way to communicate a diversion while the display caught up.

## What we left with

I synthesized the field evidence and created the interaction diagrams. The client’s communications team owned the operating protocol. We delivered tested language examples and a staged plan for piloting the revised disruption copy on one corridor. The suggested pilot would track where riders still hesitate during a disruption.

I took the slow-update problem into [Transit Board](/work/transit-board/), a working prototype that distinguishes the age of the last dispatch from the current connection status.
